What this calculator assumes

Most ESL ROI calculators hide their constants. Here are ours, with the reasoning, so you can argue with them.

The formula behind the number

Everything above collapses into one line. If you want to reproduce the result in a spreadsheet, this is all you need:

Payback (months) = 12 × C ÷ ( F × (m ÷ 60) × e × W )
C = all-in cost per labelled position ($9)
F = price changes per label per year (your input)
m = minutes per manual re-tag (1.5)
e = labour-reduction rate (0.85)
W = fully-loaded hourly wage (your input)

Worked example at the default settings: 12 × 9 ÷ (25 × 1.5/60 × 0.85 × 18) = about 11 months. Drop the wage to the US federal minimum of $7.25 and the same hardware takes roughly 28 months — on identical stores, identical labels.

Why store count and SKU count barely move the answer

Notice what is not in the formula: the number of stores and the number of labels. They cancel out. A bigger store buys proportionally more labels, but it also saves proportionally more re-pricing labour, so both sides of the ratio scale together.

The sliders for stores and SKUs still exist because they set the absolute size of the investment and the annual saving, which is what a finance team needs to see. But if you are only asking "how long until this pays for itself", the answer is decided by cost per label, price-change frequency and local wage — not by how big your estate is. The full derivation is in our guide on how to calculate ESL ROI.

What the number deliberately leaves out

Four sources of value are excluded. Each of them is genuine; none of them is in the figure above:

  • Pricing accuracy. Shelf-versus-register mismatches trigger refunds, goodwill gestures and, in some jurisdictions, regulatory penalties. Electronic labels driven from a single price source largely remove this class of error.
  • Paper and consumables. Label stock, toner and printer maintenance disappear. Small per store, non-trivial across an estate.
  • Markdown execution. When re-pricing costs almost nothing, you can mark down perishables in the afternoon rather than writing them off at close. This is often the largest excluded item — and the hardest to forecast honestly.
  • Adjacent labour. Labels with locate functions cut the time spent hunting for a facing during stock-takes and pick tasks.

If the calculator already clears your hurdle rate on labour alone, these four make the case stronger. If it does not, they are where the rest of the case has to come from — and they need evidence from your own operation, not from a vendor slide.

How to sanity-check your result

  • Under 12 months? Check your price-change frequency first. Most estates over-state how often an individual label changes. A store that runs 400 promotions a week is not changing every one of its 8,000 labels 400 times.
  • Over 36 months? That is a legitimate outcome in a low-churn, low-wage operation. It means labour alone will not carry the case, and you should model accuracy and markdown benefits explicitly rather than hope.
  • Wage input. Use fully-loaded cost, not the headline rate. Statutory minimums are legal floors before employer on-costs, which commonly add 15–30%.
  • Multi-country estates. Run each market separately. Wage spreads inside a single country can be more than 2×, which is enough to double or halve payback on identical hardware.
